- Choose a dataset of interest from the [City of Toronto’s Open Data Portal](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/data-research-maps/open-data/) or [Ontario’s Open Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/). 
- Using Python and one other data visualization software (Excel or free alternative, Tableau Public, any other tool you prefer), create two distinct visualizations from your dataset of choice.  
- For each visualization, describe and justify: 
    Visualization 1:
    > What software did you use to create your data visualization?
    I used Python for the full workflow: data loading/cleaning with pandas, and plotting/export with matplotlib (line chart).
    > Who is your intended audience? 
    Residents of Toronto and operational staff at the City of Toronto who want a high-level view of infrastructure-related 311 demand patterns.
    > What information or message are you trying to convey with your visualization? 
    Infrastructure-related 311 requests vary across the year. A monthly time series makes it easy to see overall demand and which request types dominate (and whether some categories peak at specific times).
    > What aspects of design did you consider when making your visualization? How did you apply them? With what elements of your plots? 
    - Chart choice: Since the key feature is change over time, I aggregated to monthly counts and used a line chart to emphasize trends and seasonality.
    - Reducing clutter: I plotted only the top request categories (plus an overall total), to avoid overplotting and unreadable legends.
    - Legibility: Clear title, labeled axes, and high-resolution export (dpi=300).
    > How did you ensure that your data visualizations are reproducible? If the tool you used to make your data visualization is not reproducible, how will this impact your data visualization? 
    The visualization is generated by a script that:
    1. reads the raw CSV,
    2. applies deterministic cleaning rules,
    3. filters to a clearly defined “infrastructure” subset,
    4. aggregates by month, and
    5. saves the figure to a fixed path.
    Anyone with the same input file and environment can rerun the script and regenerate the exact output.
    > How did you ensure that your data visualization is accessible?  
    - High contrast, readable font sizes, and explicit axis labels.
    > Who are the individuals and communities who might be impacted by your visualization?  
    Residents reporting infrastructure issues (e.g., roads, waste, trees, water), and City staff who use reporting patterns to anticipate workload. I would also like to point out that 311 requests reflect both conditions on the ground and differences in reporting behavior across communities.
    > How did you choose which features of your chosen dataset to include or exclude from your visualization? 
    I focused on infrastructure divisions (e.g., Transportation, Water, Solid Waste, Parks/Forestry) and excluded non-infrastructure topics (e.g., bylaw complaints). Within infrastructure, I kept the top categories so the visualization stays clear.
    > What ‘underwater labour’ contributed to your final data visualization product?
    - Cleaning a non-standard CSV (unquoted commas in some division names) so it can be parsed reliably.
    - Defining and documenting the infrastructure inclusion rule (division-based filter).
    - Choosing aggregation (monthly) and category cutoffs (top K) to balance detail vs clarity.
    


    Visualization 2:
    > What software did you use to create your data visualization?
    I used Microsoft Excel (Mac) to build a PivotTable-based heatmap using conditional formatting. I exported a cleaned and aggregated CSV from Python and used Excel to format the ward × category matrix for visual comparison.
    > Who is your intended audience? 
    Toronto residents, councillor offices, and City service teams who want to compare how infrastructure-related requests differ across wards.
    > What information or message are you trying to convey with your visualization? 
    Infrastructure-related 311 request volume varies across wards, and wards also differ in the mix of request types. The heatmap supports quick comparison across many wards and categories simultaneously.
    > > What aspects of design did you consider when making your visualization? How did you apply them?
    - Chart choice: A heatmap is compact and supports pattern-finding across a matrix.
    - Sorting: I sorted wards (e.g., by total infrastructure requests) so structure is visible rather than random.
    - Color scale: I used a single sequential color ramp to make the color values more interpretable.
    - Reducing clutter: I limited columns to the top request categories and ensured labels are readable.
    - Interpretive clarity: Titles/subtitles specify the time window and that values represent requests, not confirmed infrastructure conditions.
    > How did you ensure reproducibility? What if the tool is not reproducible?
    Excel is more manual/GUI-based than Python, so exact replication can be harder if steps aren’t documented. To mitigate this:
    - I exported a fixed processed dataset from Python (ward × category counts) so the underlying data and inclusion rules are reproducible.
    - I documented the Excel steps (PivotTable fields, filters, sorting, and conditional-formatting scale) in a markdown file.
    - I exported the final sheet as a static image so the exact result is preserved.
    Impact: if Excel steps aren’t documented, small choices (sorting, filters, binning) can change the output without a clear audit trail. Exporting the processed CSV + recording steps reduces that risk.
    > How did you ensure that your data visualization is accessible? 
    I used a single sequential color scale and kept the table to the top request categories to avoid overcrowding. 
    > Who are the individuals and communities who might be impacted by your visualization?
    Wards appearing “high” may be interpreted as having worse infrastructure or higher demand. I include a caveat that 311 request counts reflect both service needs and differences in awareness/reporting behavior across communities. This visualization is descriptive of reported requests, not a definitive measure of infrastructure quality.
    > How did you choose which features to include or exclude?
    I included ward and infrastructure request categories and excluded non-infrastructure divisions. I restricted the heatmap to the top categories to keep the matrix interpretable. I used counts (not per-capita rates) in this version to keep the workflow self-contained; I note population differences as a limitation.
    > What underwater labour contributed to your final product?
    - Creating a reproducible infrastructure filter and cleaning ward/category fields in Python.
    - Generating an analysis-ready table for Excel (ward × category, consistent labels).
    - Deciding on a stable sorting strategy and color scale so the heatmap communicates structure without distortion.
